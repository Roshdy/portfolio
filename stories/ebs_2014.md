##### The Return

I re-joined E-Business Solutions after my mandatory national service and a tour in Kuwait. They remembered me from my time there as a fresh graduate — I had apparently left a good enough impression that they brought me back as a junior web developer.

The work centred on an **Omni-Channel platform** — a system that generated UI components dynamically from MS SQL Server database configurations. For its time, it was genuinely sophisticated: a data layer handling APIs and state, a UI layer handling presentation and interactions, all modular across a structured folder architecture. No Redux, no state management libraries — _global window variables and discipline_ kept everything together. Projects came in for major Saudi banks: Alrajhi Bank, BSF, SAIB, and eventually Al-Jazira Capital.

##### Finding My Pace

Within a year I had earned a promotion to senior. The reason was simple: I consistently delivered **ahead of schedule**, at quality that regularly cleared QA with minimal review. That reputation opened doors — I was given responsibility for onboarding and mentoring junior developers, teaching CSS and jQuery standards, component patterns, and the intricacies of the Omni-Channel system. Then came the on-site business trips.

##### The BSF Sprint

My first on-site rotation was to BSF in Saudi Arabia. I was assigned **14 screens** to complete across the full trip; the team leader accompanying me had 34. The expectation, communicated explicitly, was that under-delivery was acceptable. That was all I needed to hear.

I completed my 14 screens in **three days**. The team leader reviewed everything, cleared it, and then handed me scope that was originally scheduled to be delivered a month later — back in Egypt. I kept delivering. By the time the client's CEO visited, I was building features that weren't due for weeks. The CEO pulled me aside: the client's head of IT had noticed, and the concern from eBS management was that delivering too fast was _hurting the business model_ — contracts were priced on estimated man-days, and consistently outpacing those estimates put pricing conversations at risk. It was a business logic I disagreed with, but I understood.

The BSF Head of IT sent a formal acknowledgement letter praising my performance. The meeting that followed back in Cairo went differently.

##### The SAIB Mission

My next assignment was to SAIB — this time to deploy an **IBM parallel load-balancing SMS gateway** routing messages from the bank's backend to Saudi telecom providers including STC, Mobily, and Zain. I had one briefing on the architecture. Every resource who had previously worked on the system was unavailable. The task was sold to the client as a one-week plug-and-play deployment.

On day one, the SAIB Head of IT watched every keystroke. There were complications. I leaned on my team back in Cairo for support. By end of day, the head pulled me aside and questioned whether I was the right resource for the job, having never deployed this system before. I told him directly: _"Mr. Azmy sent his best resource to bring this home. We agreed this would take two days. It hasn't been two days yet."_ He gave me until the next morning.

I went home and spent the night on calls — extracting every piece of system knowledge I could. I arrived early, armed with a plan.

I delivered the full deployment in **under two hours**.

I ran every sanity check quietly before surfacing the results. The UAT scheduled for the following day went smoothly. When the IBM queuing upgrade introduced a character encoding issue scrambling Arabic text, I diagnosed the root cause — outside my scope entirely — shared the finding with the SAIB team, and they resolved it. By the end of an extended two-week stay, _every test case was signed off and we went live_.

In the closing meeting, the Head of IT said: "On day one it looked like you were in over your head. Then you challenged us, delivered ahead of schedule, and fixed problems that weren't even yours to fix. I'll be sending Mr. Azmy a personal thank-you and requesting you by name for any future work."

##### The Thank You That Wasn't

I returned home expecting a conversation about the outcome. What I got instead was a question about why I had told the client it was my first time deploying the system. The logic put to me: I should have named other banks as prior experience, whether or not it was true.

I don't lie. And I had delivered — the client had said so in writing. The conversation ended there.

That meeting made one thing clear: I needed to find somewhere my pace and my principles were both valued, not managed down. My performance in the months that followed reflected that.
